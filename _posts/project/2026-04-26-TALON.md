Every aircraft has landing gear. It deploys for takeoff and landing, and the rest of the flight it just sits there — dead weight. So I asked: **what if the landing gear could do something useful while the drone is in the air?**

That's TALON: a simulated quadrotor whose two landing-gear jaws double as its gripper, running a complete pick-and-deliver mission — approach, dock, climb, delivery — with no human at either end.

Across 384 randomized missions the pipeline completes **69.0%**, and the last phase records the most failures. This post is about why that per-phase breakdown is misleading, and about the single quantity — the **grasp depth** — that actually governs where the missions are lost.

**GitHub**: [github.com/leesg0107/TALON](https://github.com/leesg0107/TALON)

---

## The System

A mission has four phases, each guarded by an acceptance test before control passes on:

```
[APPROACH]  ──→  [DOCK]  ──→  [CLIMB]  ──→  [DELIVERY]
 RL (PPO)      analytical     analytical      RL (PPO)
```

- **Approach**: an RL policy (PPO, trained in Isaac Lab) flies through waypoints to a position above the payload.
- **Dock**: an analytical PID controller aligns, descends, and commands the jaws shut. (A learned policy for this phase proved far less reliable in earlier development — the analytical controller commits 383 of 384 baseline missions.)
- **Climb**: a low-gain analytical controller lifts the loaded vehicle to 1.0 m.
- **Delivery**: a second RL policy, trained with the payload already grasped, flies to the delivery waypoints.

Control and physics run at 150 Hz. The vehicle is 1.12 kg; the payload is an 80 mm cube of 0.20 kg (18% of vehicle mass) on a 500 mm pedestal. Per-mission randomization covers vehicle mass, motor thrust coefficient, and wind.

---

## The Geometry That Creates the Quantity

The jaws are landing gear that was asked to do a second job, and that shows in the numbers. At the commanded closed pose the two feet together would cover only **53 mm** of the closing axis — less than the 80 mm payload. The jaws physically cannot close around it.

So what actually happens when they shut? First, both jaws brush the payload and **centre it** between them (to within ~1 mm). Then the symmetry breaks: one jaw keeps driving, pushing the payload sideways until it can push no further, while the other jaw is forced back to its open limit. In **all 297 recorded closures the same jaw won** — even in the 135 cases where the payload's initial offset pointed the other way. The vehicle model is mirror-symmetric, so the selection lives in the contact solver's deterministic tie-breaking.

How far the winning jaw drives after contact is the **grasp depth *s***. It matters because of a threshold:

- **Engaged (s > 25 mm)**: the jaw's foot passes underneath the payload, which then rests on it — an L-shaped grip (a shelf under the bottom, a wall against the side).
- **Not engaged (s < 15 mm)**: the foot stops short, and the payload is held against the jaw face by friction alone.

Nothing in the pipeline distinguishes the two. The acceptance test at the dock–climb boundary checks containment, position, tilt, and body rate — it never asks what the jaws did to the payload.

## The Losses Are Inherited, Not Generated Where They're Recorded

Sorting 383 accepted handoffs — all of which passed one and the same acceptance test — by grasp depth:

| Group | n | Completed | Payload lost in delivery |
|-------|---|-----------|--------------------------|
| Engaged (s > 25 mm) | 220 | **79.1%** | 4.5% |
| Mid (15–25 mm) | 55 | 72.7% | 3.6% |
| Not engaged (s < 15 mm) | 108 | **50.9%** | 24.1% |

A 28.2-point gap in mission completion between handoffs the pipeline cannot tell apart. Shallow grasps drop their payload in flight **5.3 times as often**.

And the quantity is frozen early: measured at both ends of the climb, the grasp depth is carried forward essentially unchanged (r = 0.9994, mean absolute change 0.055 mm; one mission in 341 changes group). A phase handed a shallow grasp has nothing left to recover with. The delivery phase records the most failures, but it didn't make them — it inherited them from a contact event two phases earlier.

One caveat the data can't resolve: the delivery policy was trained on a state bank that was 99.2% engaged (mean depth 45.8 mm), while the live pipeline hands it a mean of 25.8 mm with 29.9% not engaged. So the delivery failures mix a physical component with a training-coverage component — both hidden for the same reason: no test reads *s*.

## What Predicts Engagement

Three quantities readable at the instant the close command is issued predict whether a grasp will end engaged:

1. **Strut-axis misalignment |ex|** — with a threshold fixed by the part drawings *before* the data existed: the jaw members' inner faces sit 44 mm from centre, the payload half-width is 40 mm, leaving **4 mm** of misalignment before a member can foul the payload. The data confirmed it: 90.5% engagement inside the tolerance vs 42.5% outside (p = 2 × 10⁻⁹).
2. **Vertical clearance h** — acting through arrival momentum: a jaw released from higher up swings in faster and drives deeper.
3. **Initial offset s₀** — the strongest single predictor (AUC 0.798), even though the closure erases the offset itself before the arrest begins. It correlates strongly with h (r = 0.68); both look like readings of the same unsettled approach.

A logistic classifier on all three reaches an out-of-fold AUC of 0.883. The mean misalignment of this dock controller is 13.4 mm — more than three times the 4 mm the geometry allows.

One practical note: the grasp depth needs the payload pose, which a real vehicle doesn't have. But the advancing jaw's **arrest angle β** correlates with the depth at ρ = −0.91 and needs only a joint encoder — that's what an onboard version of this measurement would read.

## Which Quantity Governs Which Phase

Grasp depth isn't the only thing crossing the interface — the vehicle also arrives with an attitude, and stratifying by both resolves an apparent paradox (shallow grasps actually survive the *climb* more often):

- **Tilt dominates the climb**: within engaged grasps, high tilt raises climb loss from 5.3% to 57.6%.
- **Depth dominates delivery**: within low tilt, shallow grasps lose their payload at 50.0% vs 7.3% for engaged.

There's a mechanical duality behind this: the grasp depth is the payload's distance off the centreline, so a *deep* grasp is also an *eccentric* load — depth secures the payload for delivery while its eccentricity loads the attitude loop during the climb. The same number read twice, pulling in opposite directions.

## Interventions That Didn't Work (and Why That's the Finding)

Three interventions available without retraining any policy, all acting at or after the close command:

| Arm | Completed | Completed \| accepted |
|-----|-----------|----------------------|
| Baseline | 69.0% | 69.2% |
| Shaped close + depth gate | 38.0% | 88.5% |
| Re-grasp (up to 3 attempts) | 63.0% | 75.2% |

Each arm did what it was designed to do — delivery-phase loss fell from 18.0% to 1.8% under gating — but **none raised mission completion**. The failures didn't disappear; they moved to earlier phases (dock-phase loss rose from 0.3% to 16.1% under re-grasping) or into the rejection column. What the interventions trade is throughput for conditional reliability. The quantity is fixed by a contact event; everything applied after that event can only filter, not fix.

---

## The General Lesson

A pipeline guarded by boolean acceptance tests can be losing missions to a continuous quantity that none of those tests reads. The procedure that finds it transfers, even if the numbers don't:

1. **Measure** a candidate quantity where control passes on.
2. **Check** whether the phases that follow leave it alone.
3. **Sort** their outcomes by it.
4. **Work back** to what fixed its value.

Limitations worth stating: everything is in simulation, with one gripper, one payload, one contact model — and the symmetry breaking that produces the depth spread has only been observed in that one model. Release and placement are out of scope. Next steps: raise the dock controller's descent setpoint (moving the clearance term directly), retrain the delivery policy on the pipeline's real handoff distribution, and replicate the closure under a different contact solver.
